Reviewers and auditors ask the same few questions about every control. Preparing an answer to each one, with the evidence to back it, makes a review shorter and avoids follow-up requests. Use this as a checklist in the week before a review. Your team's written procedure is what gets tested; the "What good looks like" criteria on TRAC's control pages describe recommended practice, not the test.

## The questions you will be asked

- **The risk.** What goes wrong if this control fails, and which systems it covers.
- **The design.** Why the control prevents or detects the problem, why it runs at this frequency, what the reviewer looks for and how big a difference counts as an exception, and what happens when one is found.
- **Who.** Who runs it, who reviews it, and why they are independent of what they check.
- **Completeness.** How you know the control sees every item, for example every user, every change or every job run.
- **Evidence.** A real example that shows each step in your written procedure, with dates visible.
- **Reports.** If the control relies on a report or query, how you know its output is complete and accurate. See [Reports used in controls](/guides/can-you-trust-the-report/).
- **Vendors.** If a vendor runs part of the system, which of its controls you rely on and when you last reviewed its SOC report.
- **The period.** Evidence for every run across the whole period, not only recent ones.
- **Changes.** Whether anything about the control, the system or the people running it changed during the period.

## What helps to have ready

- Your written procedure or runbook, as it stood during the period.
- The source of the population: the report, query or system list the control starts from, and how it is produced.
- Evidence for every run in the period, saved in the shared evidence location with the date of each run visible, and the last few easy to open.
- The exception log: what the control found, who followed up and how each item was closed.
- A note of any change to the control during the period, with the date and the reason.
- For a control that relies on a report, the evidence that the report was checked for completeness and accuracy.

## During the review

- Answer the question asked. Short, direct answers lead to fewer follow-up questions.
- Show rather than describe. Open the system or the evidence instead of explaining from memory.
- If you don't know an answer, say so and follow up with it. Don't guess.
- Keep a list of every request and the date you answered it.
- Make sure the person who runs the control is available. A reviewer often asks them directly.

## After the review

- Close out every follow-up request on your list.
- If the review found a gap, record what will change, who owns it and when it will be done.
- Update the procedure or runbook if the review showed it was unclear or out of date.

If you're unsure, Tech Risk can help.
