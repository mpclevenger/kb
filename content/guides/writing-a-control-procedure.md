A control procedure explains how your team runs a control, so that anyone covering for you can run it the same way and a reviewer can see it works. A good one is short, starts from the risk, and is organized by what the control needs to achieve rather than by who does what.

## What a procedure is for

- It shows which risk the control addresses, so everyone knows why it matters.
- It lets someone new, or a backup, run the control correctly the first time.
- It tells a reviewer what to expect, so the evidence matches the description.
- It keeps the control consistent when people, tools or systems change.

TRAC's control pages describe recommended practice. Your procedure is where your team writes down what it actually does on its systems, and that is what reviewers check against.

## Start from the risk and the objective

- Open with the risk in one or two sentences: what could happen, to which systems or data, and why it matters. TRAC's risk statement is a good starting point.
- State the control objective: the outcome the control is designed to achieve. For example, "Only approved changes reach production."
- If the control has several parts, give each part its own objective and organize the procedure around them. A user access review might have three: the list is complete, every account is checked by the right person, and removals are completed.

## What to include for each objective

- **Scope:** which systems, environments and populations are covered, and anything deliberately left out, with the reason.
- **Who:** the role that performs it, the role that reviews it, and the backup for each. Use roles rather than names.
- **When:** how often it runs, and what triggers it if it is event-driven.
- **Steps:** short, numbered steps someone else could follow, including where to find the population and how to check it is complete.
- **Review detail:** for a review, what the reviewer looks at, what counts as an exception (for example, any access not on the approved list) and how the review is evidenced.
- **Reports used:** the report or query the control starts from, and how its completeness is shown.
- **Exceptions:** who decides what happens to each exception, and how it is recorded.
- **Evidence:** exactly what to keep from each run, with the date visible, and where it is saved.

## Keep it readable

- Aim for one or two pages. Link to runbooks for detailed technical steps instead of copying them in.
- Use the same headings in every procedure your team writes, so readers know where to look.
- Write in plain words. Define any system names or abbreviations the first time.
- Add a screenshot or example only where it removes doubt about what good evidence looks like.

## Keep it current

- Name an owner for the procedure and set a review date, at least once a year and whenever the control, system or team changes.
- Keep a short change history: the date, what changed and who approved it.
- Every statement in a procedure can be tested, so include only what the team does every time, and keep extra good practice in a separate section.
- If the procedure and practice differ, update whichever is wrong straight away and note the date. Reviewers may look at both versions.
