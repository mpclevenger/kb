The words below come up in control reviews and audit conversations. Each is explained in plain words that keep the audit meaning. Use this page when a term is new or when two people seem to mean different things by it.

## The terms

**Compensating control.** A different control that covers the same risk when the usual control is missing or can't run as designed. It needs to be as specific and as regularly evidenced as the control it stands in for, and done by someone independent of the activity.

**Complementary user entity controls.** Controls a vendor expects its customers to run for the vendor's own controls to work, such as reviewing your own users. They are listed in the vendor's SOC report, and each should map to a firm control with a named owner.

**Deficiency.** A control that is missing, badly designed or didn't operate as intended, so it can't reliably prevent or find the problem it exists for. After testing, the firm decides how serious a deficiency is.

**Key control.** A control the firm has chosen to rely on to address a risk to its financial reporting. Key controls are the ones testers test, so they need to run exactly as written, every time, with evidence. In TRAC, Key is the first of four control levels; the others are Non-key, Compensating and Supporting.

**Period of reliance.** The part of the year the firm relies on a control, usually the whole financial year. A new or fixed control has to run for a while before it can be relied on, so changes late in the year need planning.

**Population.** The complete list of items a control applies to over a period, such as every access grant, production change or job run. Testers choose their samples from it, so it must come from a system and be complete.

**Report used in a control.** Any report, list or data extract a control works from, such as a user list or a list of production changes. The control is only as good as the report, so you need to show it is complete and accurate (see "Reports used in controls").

**Risk driver.** A condition that makes a risk more likely or its impact larger, such as many systems keeping their own user lists or many people holding production access.

**Risk statement.** A short description of what could go wrong, written as what happens to which systems or data, what can cause it ("This can happen when...") and what it could lead to ("The result could be...").

**Sample.** The items testers pick from the population and examine in detail to judge whether the control worked. The more often a control runs, the larger the sample.

**Test exception.** A sample item where the control did not work as designed, such as a change with no approval. It is different from an approved exception, which is a planned departure from how a control normally runs (see "When an exception is appropriate").

**Tolerance.** The amount of a risk, or of a difference such as a mismatch in totals, that is accepted without action. It is set in advance and written down, and anything beyond it is investigated or escalated.

If you're unsure, Tech Risk can help.
