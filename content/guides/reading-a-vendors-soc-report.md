---
title: Reading a vendor's SOC report
section: run
order: 1
---
A SOC report is an independent accountant's account of how a vendor's controls were designed and whether they worked. If your system depends on a vendor, such as a cloud provider or hosted service, the vendor's controls are part of your control story. Reviewing the report is the job of the owner of the system or service that relies on the vendor. Each year, get the latest report, work through the checks below, and record what you found and what you did about it.

## Is it the right kind of report?

- **Kind.** For anything that feeds financial reporting, the report you need is a SOC 1, which covers controls relevant to financial reporting. A SOC 2 covers security and availability and may not cover what SOX needs. If only a SOC 2 exists, record what else you rely on.
- **Type.** For SOX in-scope systems a type 2 report, covering how controls worked over a period, is expected. A type 1 report describes design at a single date and doesn't show the controls worked.
- **Right report for you.** Vendors often issue several reports for different services. Confirm it covers the one you use.
- **Latest.** Use the current report. An old one tells you little about today.

## The period it covers, and any gap

- Most of the time you rely on the vendor should fall inside the report's period. Compare its dates to your control period.
- If the report ends before your period does, ask the vendor for a gap letter (also called a bridge letter). It is the vendor's own statement that nothing significant changed, and isn't tested, so it suits short gaps of a few months. For longer gaps, record what else you rely on.
- Keep the letter with the report.

## Does the scope match what you use?

- The services, products, systems and locations you use are named in the report.
- If a service you use is missing, the report does not cover it. Record what else you rely on instead.

## Exceptions and the vendor's response

- Find the tests where the testers noted an exception or deviation.
- Read the vendor's response and what changed.
- Decide whether any exception affects your use. Record the decision, who made it, and what you did about it, such as an extra check on your side or a follow-up with the vendor.
- An opinion that is not clean, or many exceptions in a key area, needs a decision from you right away, not at the next annual review.

## Carve-outs and the controls left to the firm

- **Carved-out providers.** The vendor may depend on another provider that the report does not cover, such as a hosting company. Note which, and whether that provider has its own report.
- **Complementary user entity controls.** These are the controls the vendor expects the firm to run, such as reviewing your own users or restricting who can use admin access. List each one, map it to a control that runs at the firm, and name its owner.
- If you cannot map one, it is a gap. Fix it or record the decision to accept it.

## What to keep

- The report and the gap letter, with the date received.
- Your completed checks: scope, period, exceptions and carve-outs.
- What you decided about each exception, and what you did.
- The list of complementary user entity controls, each mapped to a firm control and its owner.
- The reviewer's name and date. Review the report soon after it arrives and before year-end.
- A reminder to refresh when the next report is due.

If you're unsure, Tech Risk can help.
