A test exception means that, for at least one sample, the auditors couldn't see the control working as designed: a change with no approval, a review signed off late, a leaver whose access stayed open. It isn't automatically a failed audit or a finding against your team. What happens next depends mostly on understanding why it happened, and on how quickly and clearly you respond.

This is a different thing from an approved exception, which is a planned departure from how a control normally runs (see [When an exception is appropriate](/guides/when-an-exception-is-appropriate/)). Both terms are in [Key terms](/guides/key-terms/).

## What an exception does and doesn't mean

- **It's about specific samples.** The auditors are saying what they could and couldn't see in the evidence for those items, not that the whole control is broken.
- **They'll want to know whether it's a one-off.** They may test more samples, or ask about the rest of the period.
- **Seriousness is decided afterward.** The firm, with the auditors, decides whether the exception points to a deficiency and how serious it is. That depends on the cause, how widespread it is, and whether anything actually went wrong. Many exceptions turn out to be minor once they're understood.

## In the first few days

- **Make sure you understand exactly what was found.** Ask which samples, which period, and which part of the control the auditors couldn't see evidence of.
- **Check whether evidence already exists that they haven't seen.** If it does, share it promptly with its original date. Evidence is never created, changed or backdated after the fact. If it doesn't exist, say so and explain what happened.
- **Correct facts, don't argue the result.** If the sample was outside the control's scope or the population, explain why with evidence. Otherwise, accept it and focus on the cause.
- **Let your manager and the control owner know,** so nobody is surprised later.

## Finding the cause

Most exceptions come from one of a few causes. Knowing which one it is shapes the fix.

- **The control ran, but the evidence wasn't kept.** The review happened in a meeting or a chat, and nothing was saved.
- **It ran late.** The approval came after the change went in, or the review missed its window.
- **It didn't run.** The usual person was away and no backup was named, or the step was forgotten.
- **The control doesn't fit how the work is done.** The written procedure describes a process the team doesn't actually follow.
- **The list it ran on was incomplete.** Some changes, users or jobs never made it into the population the control worked from.

Then check the extent yourself, before anyone asks: is it this one sample, one person, one month, or a pattern? Knowing the answer first lets you describe it accurately.

## What the auditors will want to know

- What happened, and the cause.
- How far it goes: how many items, which period, which systems.
- Whether anything actually went wrong because of it, such as an unapproved change that caused a problem or access that was misused.
- Whether another control would have caught the problem anyway, such as a later reconciliation or monitoring alert.
- What you're changing, and by when.

Clear, factual answers to these usually do more for the outcome than anything else.

## Fixing it

- **Fix the instance.** For example, have the change reviewed now, or remove the access that should have gone. Record this as a fix made afterward, not as the original control.
- **Fix the cause.** Update the procedure, name a backup performer, automate the evidence, or tighten the population. See [Writing a control procedure](/guides/writing-a-control-procedure/).
- **Turn the fix into actions that get done.** Small, owned and dated; see [Writing an action item that gets done](/guides/writing-an-action-item-that-gets-done/).
- **Allow time for the fix to prove itself.** A changed control usually has to run correctly for a while before it can be tested again, so the sooner the fix is in, the better.

## Helping it not happen again

- Save evidence when the control runs, with the date visible, in the shared evidence location.
- Look at your own samples before the next review; see [Before your control review](/guides/before-your-control-review-be-ready-to-answer/).
- The habits in [Working with auditors](/guides/working-with-auditors-what-trips-teams-up/) prevent most repeat exceptions.
